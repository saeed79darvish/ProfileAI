# How to Tailor a Resume to a Job Description

Tailoring a resume means rewriting your summary, reordering your bullet points, and swapping in the specific language from the job posting — not building a new resume from scratch. Done right, it takes 10 to 15 minutes per application and roughly doubles your callback rate compared to sending the same resume everywhere. Here's exactly how to do it.

## TL;DR

- Pull 8-12 keywords straight from the job posting, prioritizing skills listed under "requirements," not "nice to have."
- Rewrite your professional summary to mirror the job title and top 2 requirements.
- Reorder — don't rewrite — your bullet points so the most relevant ones sit at the top of each role.
- Match numbers and terminology exactly (if they say "Salesforce," don't write "CRM software").
- Keep one master resume and tailor a copy for each application; never edit the master.

## Why a Generic Resume Underperforms

Recruiters spend an average of 6-8 seconds on a first pass. Applicant tracking systems parse for keyword overlap before a human ever opens the file. A resume built for "Marketing Manager" roles in general will lose to a resume built for the specific "Marketing Manager, B2B SaaS" posting sitting in front of the recruiter, even if the candidate behind the generic resume is more qualified. Tailoring isn't cheating the system — it's answering the question the posting actually asked.

## Step 1: Extract the Real Requirements

Open the job description and split it into two buckets: "must-have" and "would be nice." Most postings bury this distinction in a wall of bullet points. Look for:

- Hard requirements phrased with "required," "must have," or listed first
- Repeated terms — if "stakeholder management" shows up three times, it's not filler
- The exact tools, certifications, and job titles named

Ignore boilerplate like "fast-paced environment" or "team player." Those don't map to keywords worth matching.

## Step 2: Rewrite Your Summary in 3 Sentences

Your summary (the 2-3 lines under your name) is the highest-leverage real estate on the page because it's the first thing both a human and an ATS parser see. Structure it as:

1. Your title + years of experience, matched to their job title
2. Your strongest relevant achievement, with a number
3. One or two of their top keywords, used naturally

A generic summary: "Experienced marketing professional with a passion for growth." A tailored one: "B2B SaaS marketing manager with 6 years running demand-gen campaigns, including a program that cut CAC by 34% in 2025. Background in HubSpot, ABM strategy, and cross-functional stakeholder management."

## Step 3: Reorder, Don't Rewrite, Your Bullets

This is the step people skip because it feels too simple, but it's the one that matters most. Under each role, you likely have 4-6 bullet points. Move the ones most relevant to this specific posting to the top two slots. Recruiters and ATS parsers both weight position — bullets near the top of a section read as more important, whether or not that was your intent.

Do not invent new accomplishments to match a posting. If you genuinely lack a required skill, don't fabricate it — that's a fast way to fail a technical screen or reference check.

## Step 4: Match Terminology Exactly

ATS keyword matching is largely literal. If the posting says "Salesforce," a resume that says "CRM tools" often won't register as a match, even to a human skimming quickly. Go through your bullets and swap in their exact terms wherever you legitimately have that experience. Common swaps:

| Your resume says | Posting says | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "CRM software" | "Salesforce" | Name the specific tool if you used it |
| "Led a team" | "People management" | Use their phrase if accurate |
| "Data analysis" | "SQL, Tableau" | Name the specific tools |
| "Customer-facing role" | "Account management" | Use their job function title |

## Step 5: Keep One Master, Tailor Copies

Maintain a single master resume with every bullet point you've ever written, organized by role. For each application, duplicate it and cut it down to the version that matches this posting — don't edit your master document directly, or you'll lose material you need for future applications. This is exactly the workflow Profile AI's Tailored Profiles feature automates: it takes your master profile and a job posting and generates a matched version in about 30 seconds, then lets you edit before exporting.

## How Long Should This Actually Take?

Doing this manually: 10-20 minutes per application once you have a strong master resume built. Most of that time goes into Step 1 (reading the posting closely) and Step 4 (word-swapping). If you're applying to 15-20 roles a week, that's 3-5 hours just on tailoring — which is the actual reason most job seekers stop doing it after the first few applications, even though it works.

## FAQ

**Do I really need to tailor my resume for every single job?**
For roles you actually want, yes. For high-volume, low-effort applications (mass-applying to postings you're lukewarm on), a strong generic resume matched to your general target role is a reasonable tradeoff. The data consistently shows tailored resumes get more callbacks, but tailoring has a real time cost you have to weigh against application volume.

**Will an ATS reject my resume if I don't tailor it?**
Modern ATS platforms (Workday, Greenhouse, iCIMS) don't auto-reject based on keyword score alone in most configurations — recruiters still review applications. But a low keyword match makes your resume rank lower in search and sort views, so a recruiter searching for "Salesforce" may never scroll to your application if that word isn't on it.

**Is keyword stuffing a resume a good shortcut?**
No. Stuffing keywords in white text or an invisible footer is detectable by modern ATS platforms and, more importantly, backfires the moment a human reads the resume and it doesn't match what you actually did. Match keywords only where they reflect real experience.

**How is this different from just writing a new resume for each job?**
It isn't a new resume — it's the same underlying experience, reorganized and re-worded to answer a different question. If you're rewriting your work history from scratch each time, you're doing more work than necessary and risking inconsistency across versions.
