# AI Cover Letter Generator: How to Write One in Under 5 Minutes

An AI cover letter generator works by pulling your resume content and the job posting details into a draft that hits the standard structure — why this role, why you're qualified, why this company — in about 30 seconds. The honest catch: an unedited AI draft reads generically, and recruiters who've read hundreds of them can usually tell. The 5-minute version isn't "generate and submit," it's "generate, then spend the remaining 4 minutes making it specific."

## TL;DR

- Generating a first draft takes seconds; making it not sound generic takes a few focused minutes of editing.
- The three things worth rewriting by hand: the opening line, one specific detail about the company, and the closing.
- Recruiters generally don't penalize AI-assisted cover letters — they penalize generic ones, whether AI-written or not.
- Feed the tool the actual job posting, not just a role title, for a meaningfully better first draft.
- A cover letter should add information not on your resume, not restate it in paragraph form.

## What an AI Cover Letter Generator Actually Does

Give it your resume and a job posting, and it produces a structured draft: an opening that names the role, 1-2 paragraphs connecting your background to the requirements, and a closing. It's genuinely useful for the part that's mechanically repetitive — getting from a blank page to a workable structure — and genuinely bad at the part that requires knowing something specific about you or the company that isn't in either input document.

## The 5-Minute Process

1. **Generate against the actual posting, not a generic prompt.** "Write me a cover letter for a marketing manager role" produces something usable but vague. Pasting in the real job posting produces a draft that already reflects their specific requirements and language.
2. **Rewrite the opening line.** Generated openings tend toward "I am excited to apply for the Marketing Manager position at [Company]." Replace it with something that shows you've read the posting — a specific product, a recent launch, a problem the role is clearly meant to solve.
3. **Add one detail an AI tool couldn't know.** Why this company specifically, a person you spoke with, something about their product you've actually used. This single addition does more to signal a real, non-generic letter than anything else in the document.
4. **Cut anything that just repeats your resume.** A cover letter that summarizes your resume in paragraph form wastes the recruiter's time twice. Use it to add context the resume can't hold — why you're making a career change, what draws you to this specific team, a story behind one accomplishment.
5. **Rewrite the closing.** Generated closings tend to be interchangeable ("I look forward to discussing further"). A specific closing that references something from the letter itself reads as more deliberate.

## Do Recruiters Penalize AI-Written Cover Letters?

Most recruiters report not caring how a cover letter was drafted — they care whether it's specific and shows real engagement with the role. What gets flagged in practice isn't "AI-assisted," it's "generic," and those aren't the same thing. A fully human-written cover letter that never mentions anything specific to the company reads exactly as poorly as an unedited AI draft with the company name swapped in. The tell isn't the tool, it's the lack of specificity.

## Where AI Drafts Go Wrong If Left Unedited

| Problem | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Generic opening | "I am excited to apply for..." | Reference something specific about the role or company |
| Resume restatement | Paragraph version of your bullet points | Add context not already on the resume |
| No company specificity | Could be sent to any company in the industry | Name a product, project, or detail unique to them |
| Overly formal tone | Reads stiffer than how you'd actually talk | Read it aloud and edit anything you wouldn't say |
| Generic closing | "I look forward to hearing from you" | Tie the closing back to something specific in the letter |

## When a Cover Letter Is Worth Writing at All

Not every application needs one — many postings mark it optional, and a rushed, generic cover letter can do more harm than skipping it. Write one when: the posting requires it, the role is genuinely high-priority for you, or you have a specific reason for the role or company change that the resume alone doesn't explain. Skip it, or keep it to two sentences, for high-volume applications to roles that are a lower priority — that time is often better spent tailoring the resume instead (see our [tailoring guide](/blog/how-to-tailor-a-resume-to-a-job-description)).

## FAQ

**Can hiring managers tell if a cover letter was AI-generated?**
Often, yes, if it's left unedited — generic phrasing and a lack of company-specific detail are the tell, not some kind of AI-detection watermark. An edited draft with specific details is generally indistinguishable from one written entirely by hand.

**Is it dishonest to use AI to write a cover letter?**
No more than using a template, a formatting guide, or an editor's suggestions — the content still needs to accurately reflect your real experience and reasons for applying. The dishonesty risk is in the content (misrepresenting your background), not the drafting tool.

**How long should a cover letter be?**
Three to four short paragraphs, well under a page. Longer cover letters are rarely read in full; recruiters generally spend less time on a cover letter than on the resume itself.

**Do I need a different cover letter for every single job?**
The structure can stay similar, but the specific details — why this company, why this role — need to change for it to read as genuine. A cover letter that could be sent to five different companies unedited isn't doing its job.
